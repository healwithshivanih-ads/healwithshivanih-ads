## 1 · Why the weight comes back (1:00 – 2:30)

To understand regain, you need to understand what these drugs actually do.

GLP-1 is a hormone your own gut makes after you eat. It tells your brain, "we've had enough," and slows down how fast your stomach empties. Drugs like semaglutide and tirzepatide copy that signal, but much stronger and for much longer.

So the simplest way to think about it: the medicine turns the volume on hunger right down. A lot of people describe it as the "food noise" going quiet for the first time in years.

When you stop, the volume comes back up. Often to where it was before.

And there's a second layer. When you lose weight by any method, your body tries to defend the weight it was used to. Your appetite hormones shift, and your body becomes a little more efficient with energy. Researchers call this metabolic adaptation.

So if the weight comes back after stopping, please hear this: **it is not a willpower failure.** It's biology doing what biology does. The good news is that biology responds to what you do. Let me show you the numbers.

## 2 · The honest numbers (2:30 – 5:00)

Let's start with the clinical trials.

In the semaglutide trial called STEP-1, people lost about 17% of their body weight over 68 weeks. Then they stopped the drug and were followed for a year. In that year, they regained about **two-thirds** of what they'd lost.

`[STICKER]` **Semaglutide: ~⅔ of lost weight regained within 1 year of stopping.**
Source: Wilding et al., *Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism*, 2022 (STEP-1 extension).

With tirzepatide, the drug in Mounjaro and Zepbound, a trial called SURMOUNT-4 lost about 21% first. Then half the group was quietly switched to a placebo. Over the next year, that group regained about 14% of their body weight, roughly half of what they'd lost.

`[STICKER]` **Tirzepatide: ~half regained within 1 year of switching to placebo.**
Source: Aronne et al., *JAMA*, 2024 (SURMOUNT-4).

Then, in January 2026, Oxford researchers pulled together 37 studies with more than 9,000 people. For the newer drugs, weight came back at about 0.8 kg a month, almost 2 pounds, and was projected to be back at the starting point in about a year and a half.

Two more findings from that review matter just as much:

First, it wasn't just weight. Blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol, which had all improved on the drug, drifted back too, within about a year and a half.

Second, weight came back **faster** after stopping a drug than after finishing a diet-and-exercise programme. That makes sense: a programme teaches skills you keep. A drug does the work *for* you, and when it stops, it stops.

`[STICKER]` **37 studies · 9,300+ people · ~0.8 kg/month regain · back to baseline in ~1.5 years.**
Source: *The BMJ*, January 2026, systematic review and meta-analysis (University of Oxford).

So far, that sounds grim. Now here's the twist.

In March 2026, Cleveland Clinic looked at nearly 8,000 real patients, not trial volunteers. These were people who had stopped semaglutide or tirzepatide after three months to a year. The ones treated for weight had lost about 8% of their body weight. A year after stopping, they'd regained only about **half a percent**.

`[STICKER]` **Real-world: 7,938 patients · lost 8.4% · regained 0.5% a year after stopping.**
Source: Gasoyan et al., *Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism*, 2026 (Cleveland Clinic).

So how can both be true?

The researchers explained it themselves. In real life, most people didn't just stop and walk away. Many restarted, switched to another treatment, or kept working on weight with a professional. In the trials, people were switched to nothing.

To be fair, this group also took the drug for a shorter time and lost less, so it isn't a perfect comparison. But the lesson is clear:

**Stopping the drug isn't what decides your outcome. What you have in place when you stop is.**

That's the rest of this video.

## 3 · The hidden problem: what you actually lost (5:00 – 6:45)

Before the plan, there's one thing almost nobody talks about.

When you lose weight quickly, not all of it is fat. In the STEP-1 trial, a group of participants had body scans. Roughly 40% of the weight they lost was what the scan calls "lean mass".

Now, lean mass isn't only muscle. It also includes water, organs and other tissue, so that number overstates true muscle loss. Studies of tirzepatide found it closer to a quarter. But either way, a meaningful part of the weight that comes off is tissue you want to keep.

`[STICKER]` **Up to ~40% of weight lost on semaglutide showed up as "lean mass" on DXA scans (not all of it is muscle).**
Source: Wilding et al., *NEJM* 2021, STEP-1 body-composition substudy.

Here's why that matters for regain. Muscle is your metabolic engine. It burns energy at rest, it helps your body handle blood sugar, and it keeps you strong as you age.

If you lose muscle on the way down and then regain weight, the evidence on weight cycling suggests the weight that comes back tends to be more fat than muscle. So you can end up at the same number on the scale, but with a body that's weaker and less metabolically healthy than when you started.

## 4 · Your soft-landing plan (6:45 – 11:45)

I call this a soft landing. Most of these steps work best if you start **while you're still on the medicine**, not after. The drug quiets your appetite, which makes it the easiest time to build new habits. Think of it as training wheels you use to learn the bike, so you can still ride when they come off.

### Step 1 · Protein, at every meal

When your appetite is low, protein is often the first thing to drop. Yet protein is what tells your body to hold on to muscle.

In 2025, four major obesity and nutrition organisations published joint guidance for people on these medicines, putting adequate protein near the top of the list. The range most experts now use is about **1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilo of body weight per day**.

`[STICKER]` **Protein: ~1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight per day, spread across meals.**
Source: Joint advisory "Nutritional Priorities to Support GLP-1 Therapy for Obesity" (ACLM, ASN, OMA, TOS), 2025.

`⚠ CHECK` Confirm the exact protein wording in the advisory PDF (obesity.org was blocked from my research environment; the 1.2–1.6 g/kg figure is widely attributed to it).

So if you weigh 70 kilos, that's somewhere around 85 to 110 grams a day. Don't try to get that in one meal. Spread it across the day: about 25 to 30 grams at each main meal.

What does that look like? Two or three eggs. A bowl of Greek yoghurt. A palm-and-a-half of fish or chicken. A cup of cooked lentils with some tofu or paneer. A protein-rich breakfast is the one most people miss.

`[B-ROLL]` Three plates, each showing ~25–30 g of protein.

### Step 2 · Strength training, two to three times a week

Protein is the building material. Strength training is the signal to use it.

You don't need a gym membership or heavy barbells to start. Squats to a chair, wall push-ups, resistance bands, carrying shopping bags. What matters is that your muscles work against resistance two or three times a week, and that it gets a little harder over time.

Protein and strength work as a pair. Either one alone does far less than both together.

`[STICKER]` **Protein + strength training: neither works without the other.**

### Step 3 · Fibre: help your body make its own GLP-1

Remember, GLP-1 is a hormone your own gut makes. One of the ways it does this is through fibre.

When you eat fibre from beans, lentils, oats, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds, the bacteria in your gut ferment it into compounds called short-chain fatty acids. In human studies, one of these, propionate, increased the body's own release of GLP-1 and another fullness hormone called PYY, and people ate less.

`[STICKER]` **Gut bacteria turn fibre into short-chain fatty acids, which raise your body's own GLP-1 and PYY.**
Source: Chambers et al., *Gut*, 2015.

Let me be really clear: your natural GLP-1 is much weaker than the drug. Fibre will not replace the medicine. But it works *with* your own appetite system rather than against it. That's exactly what you want when the drug is no longer doing the work.

If you've seen "fibremaxxing" on social media, this is the sensible version of it: build up slowly, aim for fibre at every meal, and drink enough water.

### Step 4 · Sleep and stress

This one surprises people. In a well-known study, healthy young men slept only four hours a night for two nights. Their hunger hormone, ghrelin, went up by about 28%, their fullness hormone, leptin, went down, and their appetite for sweet and starchy foods jumped by a third or more.

`[STICKER]` **Two short nights of sleep: hunger hormone ↑28%, cravings for sweet and starchy food ↑33–45%.**
Source: Spiegel et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2004. (Small study; later research is mixed, but most points the same way.)

`⚠ CHECK` Optional second sticker: Al Khatib et al., *European Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, 2017 (meta-analysis, short sleep → ~385 extra kcal/day). Verify the figure before using it.

Chronic stress does something similar. So if your sleep and stress are a mess when the drug's volume knob comes back up, you're fighting your appetite on two fronts. Protect your sleep, especially in the months around stopping.

### Step 5 · Learn your hunger while it's quiet

This is the skill almost nobody practises. While you're on the medicine and the food noise is quiet, notice what it feels like to eat until you're comfortably satisfied, not stuffed. Notice which meals keep you full and which leave you hunting for snacks an hour later. Eat slowly. Stop when you're satisfied.

Write down what you learn. Those are your notes for later, when the volume comes back up.

### Bonus · Set a "return line"

Last one, and it's simple. Weigh yourself about once a week, and decide in advance on a "return line", say two or three kilos above where you are now.

If you cross it, that's not failure. It's your signal to act: tighten up the protein and fibre, get back to strength training, check your sleep, and book a conversation with your doctor or coach. Catching regain at two kilos is a very different conversation from catching it at ten.

`⚠ CHECK` Optional support: the US National Weight Control Registry found that most long-term maintainers weigh themselves at least weekly (Wing & Phelan, *AJCN* 2005). Verify the wording before adding a sticker.

## 5 · Talk to your prescriber (11:45 – 12:45)

Please don't stop on your own. Have a conversation first. Here are some questions worth taking with you:

1. **"Should I come off gradually rather than all at once?"** Some clinics taper the dose down over several weeks. Early observational data looks promising, and proper trials are under way, but the evidence isn't settled yet.
2. **"Is a lower maintenance dose an option for me?"**
3. **"What should we monitor after I stop?"** Remember, blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol can drift back too, not just weight.
4. **"What's our plan if I start regaining?"**

## 6 · If you've already stopped and you're regaining (12:45 – 13:45)

If you've already stopped and the scale is going up, first: you didn't fail. Remember the numbers. Most people in the trials regained a large part of their weight. That's the biology, not you.

Second, it's not too late. Start with the two that protect your body most: protein at every meal and strength training twice a week. Then add fibre and look honestly at your sleep. And talk to your doctor about your options. There's no shame in deciding together that a medicine is part of your long-term plan, just like any other medicine for a long-term condition.
