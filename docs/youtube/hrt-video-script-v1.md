## 3. Where the fear came from (1:30–4:00)

[SLIDE: Timeline · 2002 · 2003 · 2025 · 2026]

In 2002, a large US study called the Women's Health Initiative, or WHI, published early results. The headlines said hormone therapy raised the risk of breast cancer, heart disease and stroke.

Women everywhere stopped. Use of hormone therapy dropped by around half almost overnight. In 2003, a black box warning was added. That's the strongest warning a medicine can carry.

But here's what the headlines left out.

[SLIDE: The women in the WHI study]
- Average age: early 60s
- Many were 10+ years past menopause
- Older types of hormones

The women in that study were mostly well past menopause. They weren't the women in their 40s and early 50s who are struggling with perimenopause symptoms right now.

Over the next two decades, researchers re-analysed the data. A clearer picture emerged: *when* you start matters a lot.

---

## 4. What changed in 2025–26 (4:00–6:00)

On 10 November 2025, the FDA announced it would remove the black box warning from menopause hormone therapy.

By February 2026, it had approved new labels for six products, dropping the boxed warnings for heart disease, breast cancer and probable dementia.

[STICKER: Prometrium · Divigel · Cenestin · Enjuvia · Bijuva · Estring]

The current view is this: for most women who start before age 60, or within 10 years of menopause, the benefits outweigh the risks.

[SLIDE: "The timing window" · Before 60 · or within 10 years of menopause]

Now, what did *not* change.

Hormone therapy is still a medical decision. It's still individual. Your personal history, your family history, your blood pressure, your clotting risk, all of that still matters. Removing a warning label doesn't mean it's right for everyone.

What changed is that the conversation is now open. You're allowed to ask.

## 5. When fear makes the decision for you (6:00–7:30)

Let me tell you about a client of mine.

She had breast cancer in her family, so her doctor said no to HRT. End of conversation.

For two years she struggled. Every month, three or four days before her period, she would completely crash. Her bleeding swung between very scanty and very heavy. Her energy was a roller coaster.

Eventually she saw a gynaecologist who looked at *her* history, *her* risks, and *her* quality of life, and decided this was no way to live. They started HRT together.

It took three to four months to settle. Today she's very stable.

I'm not telling you this so you ignore your doctor if they say no. Family history of breast cancer is a real factor, and for some women HRT genuinely isn't the right choice.

The lesson is: you deserve a proper assessment of *your* situation. Not a blanket no based on fear. And if you don't get that, it's okay to ask for a second opinion.

## 6. How to talk to your doctor (7:30–10:00)

So how do you walk into that appointment and actually get heard?

**Step one: bring numbers, not just feelings.**

"I just don't feel like myself" is real. But it's easy to dismiss. I know, because it was dismissed for me.

There's a simple, validated questionnaire called the Menopause Rating Scale. It's 11 questions covering things like hot flushes, sleep, mood, joint aches and bladder issues. You score each one from 0 to 4.

[SLIDE: Menopause Rating Scale · 11 symptoms · scored 0–4 · total out of 44]

Fill it in before your appointment. Then you're walking in with a score, not just a story. And you can repeat it later to see what's changing.

[STICKER: Free MRS tracker + question list · link below]

**Step two: take these questions with you.**

[SLIDE: Questions to ask your doctor]
1. Based on my age and when my periods changed, am I within the timing window?
2. Given my personal and family history, what are *my* specific risks?
3. What types and forms of hormone therapy are there, and how do they differ for someone like me?
4. If we start, how will we monitor it, and when will we review it?
5. If hormone therapy isn't right for me, what are my other options?

Write these down. Doctors are busy. A clear list helps both of you.

## 7. What you can do yourself, with or without HRT (10:00–14:00)

Whatever you and your doctor decide, there's a lot in your own hands. Lifestyle doesn't replace medical care. But it makes everything work better.

[STICKER: These work *alongside* medical care, not instead of it]

**Food**

Two things matter most.

First, protein. As estrogen drops, we lose muscle more easily. Aim for a good portion of protein at every meal: eggs, dal, paneer, tofu, fish, chicken, Greek yoghurt.

Second, fibre. Your gut bacteria help process and clear estrogen. Researchers call this part of the microbiome the "estrobolome." Vegetables, legumes, whole grains, seeds: feed it every day.

And one thing to cut back on: alcohol. It disrupts sleep, worsens hot flushes, and adds to the work your liver does clearing hormones. If you change only one thing, this is a good place to start.

[B-ROLL: a balanced plate]

**Sleep**

Perimenopause often wrecks sleep, especially that 3 a.m. wake-up.

- Get morning daylight within an hour of waking
- Keep your bedroom cool
- Stop caffeine by midday
- Keep a regular wind-down routine

Small, boring, and they work.

**Movement**

If you do one kind of exercise, make it strength training. Two or three sessions a week protects your muscle and your bones, which become more vulnerable after menopause.

You may have heard about creatine for women over 40. The research is promising but still emerging, so that's one to discuss with your doctor first.

[B-ROLL: you lifting weights]

**Stress and your nervous system**

Hormone shifts make your stress response more sensitive. So the same work deadline feels bigger than it did at 35.

Try this with me: breathe in for 4, out for 6. Five rounds. The longer exhale tells your body it's safe.

[ON SCREEN: breathing guide animation]

Do this before bed, or anytime you feel that rush.

## 8. When it all works together (14:00–15:00)

Another client of mine came to me with very high blood sugar. Her HbA1c was 14.

[STICKER: HbA1c 14 · normal is below 5.7]

She followed her plan with me: food, movement, sleep, stress. At the same time, her gynaecologist started her on HRT.

Over a year and a half, her doctors were able to take her off all her medicines.

I want to be really clear: HRT didn't fix her diabetes, and neither did I alone. She did the work, and her medical team made the medical decisions. This is what it looks like when everyone works together.
